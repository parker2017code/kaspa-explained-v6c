Fast blocks collide.

A new block needs a moment to reach every miner. Two found in that moment can’t share one chain. One gets thrown away.

The work behind the thrown-away block is wasted. Bitcoin avoids collisions by making blocks rare. Kaspa keeps both.

Show me more: the collision math

Blocks arrive at random, like raindrops: a Poisson process with rate λ. Two collide when the next one is found before the first has spread, which takes delay d. The share a chain discards grows with λ × d. At Kaspa’s ten a second and a 250 ms delay, λd = 2.5 and a chain would discard about 71% of blocks. Checked against the PHANTOM/GHOSTDAG paper, eprint.iacr.org/2018/104.

Keep every block.
Agree on one order.

Each Kaspa block points at every recent block its miner could see. The rule called then reads that web the same way on every node and lines all of it up.

This uses the last ten seconds of the real network, not a simulation.

Show me more: how the order is built

Each block names one selected parent, the parent with the most blue work behind it. Following those links gives a spine through the web, the selected chain (brighter squares). Each chain block merges the blocks it saw that its selected parent had not: its mergeset. The order is: everything before, then the mergeset sorted by accumulated work, then the chain block itself.

This page rebuilds that order in your browser from fields the node publishes for every block: the chain flag, selected parent, mergeset and blue work. A node’s own virtual-chain call is the authority; this is a view of it. Blocks the network hasn’t merged yet are shown as waiting.

A block stays blue if, among the blocks it could not have seen, at most k are blue. Kaspa sets k from the block rate and a worst-case network delay. Mainnet runs GHOSTDAG; DAGKnight is the planned successor that adapts to the network instead (see the race, below).

Show me more: why parallel payments don’t queue

Kaspa tracks money as separate coins, the way Bitcoin does, rather than one balance per account. Payments that land in parallel blocks don’t have to wait for each other.

Try to cheat.

You control a slice of the mining power. Mine blocks in secret, then release them and try to rewrite the order. The honest network keeps going without you.

A teaching model with k = 3 and two honest blocks per round. The real network uses a k set from its block rate.

Show me more: why the hidden blocks turn red

While you hid, the honest network kept making blocks your branch never saw. That gap is your blocks’ anticone. A block stays blue only if at most k blue blocks sit in its anticone. Honest blocks, published at once, have small anticones. Yours has a huge one, so it is colored red and ordered after the honest ones. Red blocks still count; they just can’t jump the queue.

Show me more: the numbers behind “seconds”

For Kaspa, Sompolinsky gives figures on X (February 26, 2026): at 10 blocks a second, a 37% attacker can fake the majority signal within a second with probability 12%; at 100 blocks a second that drops to 0.3%. He also writes that Bitcoin reaches censorship resistance in about an hour and Kaspa reaches the same level in 1 to 3 seconds. Post, post. That is why core wants more blocks per second, not for throughput. “Scalability is boring,” in his words.

Satoshi’s formula, for Bitcoin.

Section 11 of the Bitcoin white paper, one block per ten minutes. Exact for Bitcoin’s model; it does not apply to a DAG as is.

Show me more: when the internet breaks

GHOSTDAG fixes its safety margin k in advance, from an assumed worst-case delay. If the real network is much slower, a fixed margin can be too tight. DAGKnight, Kaspa’s planned next consensus, measures the network as it goes: fast when the internet is calm, slower but still safe when it splits. Core developers merged more DAGKnight code toward testnet in September 2026; it is not on mainnet.

No premine. No sale.
No insiders.

On November 23, 2021, Kaspa started with an empty ledger. Every coin since was paid to whoever mined a block, on the same terms for everyone.

The company that funded early research, DAGLabs, mined an estimated 2.5 to 3% of supply in the first months, in the open, like any other miner.

Share of supply held by founders, companies or foundations at launch.

Show me more: where these numbers come from

Kaspa: the node’s genesis file ships an empty coin set. Ethereum: about 12M of the ~72M genesis ETH (16.7%) went to founders, early contributors and the Ethereum Foundation; the rest was sold in the 2014 crowdsale. Cardano: IOHK, Emurgo and the Cardano Foundation held 11.52% of the 45B maximum. XRP: all 100B were created for Ripple and its founders.

No halving cliffs

The block reward halves every year in twelve monthly steps of about 5.6%. Supply never passes about 28.7 billion KAS.

Who pays the miners later

Today new coins pay almost all of it. As they taper, fees have to grow. It is the most common serious doubt about Kaspa, and a fair one.

A coin can carry
its own rules.

On June 30, 2026, the Toccata upgrade went live. A Kaspa coin can now say how it may be spent, and every miner checks that rule the way it checks a signature.

“Only spendable after March.” “Only to my cold wallet.” These rules are covenants. They run on Kaspa itself, with no separate chain. On October 27 there were 1,689 active covenants on mainnet.

Rob the vault. You have the owner’s key.

The coin may go only to the owner’s cold wallet, or anywhere after the lock date.

Pick a move.

Show me more: how covenants work

Toccata turned on four proposals: KIP-17 lets a script check the transaction spending it, so a coin can demand a matching next output; KIP-20 gives covenants an ID that consensus tracks, so lookalikes can’t be forged; KIP-16 lets a script check a zero-knowledge proof instead of re-running the work; KIP-21 adds sequencing commitments so an app can prove its own lane. Activation was at DAA score 474,165,565.

SilverScript, released as v1 in September, lets developers write these rules in readable code. What covenants can’t do yet: many users changing one shared app state at once. That is the goal of vProgs, still a prototype.

Show me more: run a node, mine

The Rust node, rusty-kaspa, runs on a normal desktop and keeps only recent history, so it does not grow forever. Small home miners can point at their own node and solo mine; at ten blocks a second, they win blocks often.

What isn’t settled.

SecurityWill fees pay for security?

New coins shrink every year. Settled by: fee income growing as the reward falls.

UseWill people use it?

Settled by: payments and covenant apps with real users, not just holders.

RoadmapIs the roadmap delivered?

Crescendo and Toccata shipped. DAGKnight and vProgs have no dates. Settled by: testnet, then mainnet.

MiningIs mining concentrated?

Most hashing comes from specialized machines and a few pools. Settled by: pool share and solo mining over time.
